CHAPTER ONE
INTRODUCTION
1.1 BACKGROUND OF STUDY
Agriculture is a fundamental pillar of economic development and food security across the world, particularly in developing countries where a large proportion of the population depends on farming for survival. The sector provides food, employment, raw materials for industries, and income generation for rural households. However, agricultural productivity continues to face serious challenges, among which plant diseases remain one of the most significant threats. Plant diseases negatively affect crop growth, reduce yield quantity and quality, and result in substantial economic losses for farmers and stakeholders along the agricultural value chain.
Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is one of the most widely cultivated and economically important vegetable crops globally. It is highly valued due to its nutritional content, including vitamins A, C, and E, minerals, antioxidants such as glycogen, and its extensive use in households and food-processing industries. Tomatoes are consumed fresh or processed into products such as paste, juice, ketchup, and sauces, making them a vital component of food systems worldwide. Due to its high demand and commercial value, tomato farming contributes significantly to income generation for small-scale and commercial farmers alike.
Despite its importance, tomato cultivation is highly vulnerable to numerous plant diseases caused by fungi, bacteria, viruses, and pests. Common tomato diseases include early blight, late blight, leaf mold, septoria leaf spot, bacterial spot, bacterial speck, tomato mosaic virus, and tomato yellow leaf curl virus. These diseases often manifest through visible symptoms on tomato leaves such as discoloration, spots, wilting, curling, and necrosis. If not detected and treated at an early stage, tomato diseases can spread rapidly across farms, leading to severe yield losses and, in extreme cases, total crop failure.
Traditionally, the identification and diagnosis of tomato plant diseases have relied on manual inspection by farmers or agricultural extension officers. This conventional approach has several limitations. First, it requires expert knowledge, which is often unavailable or inaccessible to many farmers, especially those in rural areas. Second, visual inspection is time-consuming, subjective, and prone to human error, as different diseases may exhibit similar visual symptoms. Third, large-scale tomato farms make manual monitoring impractical and inefficient. As a result, misdiagnosis is common, leading farmers to apply incorrect or excessive agrochemicals, which increases production costs, harms the environment, and poses health risks to consumers.
The rapid advancement of information and communication technology has opened new possibilities for addressing these challenges in agriculture. Image processing and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques have emerged as powerful tools for automating plant disease detection. Image processing enables the analysis of visual information from plant leaf images by extracting key features such as color variations, texture patterns, and shape irregularities that are indicative of disease symptoms. These features provide valuable information about the health status of tomato plants and form the basis for intelligent disease diagnosis systems.
In recent years, deep learning, a sub-field of machine learning, has demonstrated remarkable success in solving complex image analysis problems. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), in particular, have proven highly effective in image classification and object recognition tasks due to their ability to automatically learn hierarchical and discriminative features directly from raw images. Unlike traditional machine learning methods that rely on handcrafted feature extraction, CNN-based models learn relevant features autonomously, making them more robust and accurate for plant disease detection.
The application of deep learning to tomato disease detection has gained increasing attention among researchers due to the availability of large annotated image datasets and improved computational power. Public datasets such as PlantVillage have enabled the training and evaluation of deep learning models for various tomato diseases. In addition, the widespread use of smartphones equipped with high-resolution cameras has made it possible to capture tomato leaf images directly from farms, creating opportunities for real-time and user-friendly disease diagnosis systems.
However, despite these technological advancements, several challenges remain in developing reliable tomato disease detection models. Variations in lighting conditions, background clutter, leaf orientation, disease severity levels, and environmental factors can significantly affect image quality and model performance. Many existing models are trained on laboratory-controlled datasets, which limits their ability to generalize to real-world farming conditions. Furthermore, deep learning models often require large datasets and high computational resources, which may restrict their deployment on mobile or low-power devices commonly used by farmers.
In response to these challenges, there is a growing need for efficient, accurate, and robust artificial intelligence-based systems specifically designed for tomato disease detection. Such systems should be capable of accurately identifying tomato diseases from leaf images under varying environmental conditions while remaining computationally efficient and accessible to end users. An automated tomato disease detection system has the potential to assist farmers in early diagnosis, reduce crop losses, optimize pesticide usage, and support sustainable agricultural practices.
This project focuses on the development of an artificial model for detecting diseases in tomato plants using image processing and deep learning techniques. By leveraging convolutional neural networks and appropriate image preprocessing methods, the proposed system aims to provide an automated, reliable, and cost-effective solution for early tomato disease detection. The outcome of this research is expected to contribute to the advancement of smart agriculture and precision farming by providing a technological tool that supports timely decision-making and enhances tomato crop productivity.
In conclusion, the integration of image processing and deep learning techniques into tomato disease detection represents a promising approach to addressing one of the most pressing challenges in modern agriculture. As global food demand continues to rise due to population growth and climate change, the adoption of intelligent agricultural technologies becomes increasingly essential. This study aligns with global efforts toward sustainable agriculture by exploring the use of artificial intelligence to improve tomato plant health monitoring and ensure food security.

Tomato production plays a vital role in ensuring food security, nutritional balance, and economic sustainability for farmers and agricultural stakeholders worldwide. Despite its importance, tomato farming continues to face serious challenges arising from the frequent occurrence of plant diseases. Many tomato diseases exhibit similar visual symptoms at early stages, making accurate diagnosis difficult even for experienced farmers. Such requirements make it difficult to deploy these models on mobile devices or low-cost systems that are commonly accessible to farmers. In addition, there is a lack of locally adaptable and user-friendly tomato disease detection systems that can provide accurate results in a timely manner. Most farmers need simple, fast, and affordable diagnostic tools that can operate with minimal technical expertise. However, current solutions often fail to meet these practical requirements, limiting their adoption at the grassroots level.
Therefore, the core problem addressed in this study is the absence of an efficient, accurate, and accessible automated system for early detection of tomato plant diseases. There is a critical need for an intelligent tomato disease detection model that leverages image processing and deep learning techniques to overcome the limitations of manual inspection and existing automated approaches. Such a system should be capable of accurately identifying tomato diseases from leaf images, operate under varying environmental conditions, and be suitable for deployment on affordable platforms. Addressing this problem is essential for improving tomato crop health, enhancing agricultural productivity, and promoting sustainable farming practices.

CHAPTER TWO
LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
This chapter presents a critical review of existing literature related to tomato disease detection using image processing and deep learning techniques. The review focuses on recent studies published between 2020 and 2026, highlighting methodologies, models, datasets, and performance outcomes. The purpose of this chapter is to establish the theoretical and empirical foundation of the study, identify research trends, and reveal gaps that justify the need for the proposed system.
2.2 Tomato Plant Diseases and Agricultural Importance
Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is one of the most widely cultivated and economically important vegetable crops worldwide. However, tomato production is significantly affected by various diseases such as early blight, late blight, septoria leaf spot, bacterial spot, and leaf mold, which reduce yield and quality (FAO, 2021). Early and accurate detection of these diseases is crucial for effective disease management and food security.
Traditional disease diagnosis relies on visual inspection by farmers or agricultural experts, which is often time-consuming, subjective, and prone to errors, especially in large-scale farms (Polder et al., 2020). These challenges have motivated researchers to explore automated disease detection techniques based on image processing and artificial intelligence.
2.3 Image Processing Techniques in Plant Disease Detection
Image processing plays a foundational role in automated plant disease detection systems. Common preprocessing techniques include image resizing, color space conversion, noise reduction, contrast enhancement, and segmentation (Gandhi et al., 2021). These techniques aim to improve image quality and isolate regions of interest, such as diseased leaf areas.
Several studies have employed segmentation methods such as thresholding, k-means clustering, and region-based segmentation to extract disease-affected regions from tomato leaf images (Singh & Misra, 2020). While traditional image processing techniques can be effective, they often require handcrafted feature extraction and are sensitive to lighting conditions and background variations.
2.4 Deep Learning in Plant Disease Detection
Deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), has emerged as a powerful tool for image-based plant disease detection. CNNs automatically learn hierarchical features from raw images, eliminating the need for manual feature engineering (LeCun et al., 2015).
Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of CNN-based models in tomato disease classification. Ouhami et al. (2020) applied transfer learning using DenseNet and VGG architectures on tomato leaf images and achieved high classification accuracy. Similarly, Trivedi et al. (2021) developed a deep CNN model that successfully classified multiple tomato leaf diseases with accuracy exceeding 98%.
2.5 Transfer Learning Approaches
Transfer learning has been widely adopted to improve model performance and reduce training time, especially when labeled datasets are limited. Pre-trained models such as VGG16, ResNet50, InceptionV3, MobileNet, and EfficientNet have been fine-tuned for tomato disease detection tasks (Saeed et al., 2023).
Islam et al. (2022) reported that fine-tuning pre-trained CNN models significantly improved classification accuracy compared to training models from scratch. Lightweight models such as MobileNet and EfficientNet are particularly suitable for mobile and edge-based agricultural applications due to their reduced computational requirements (Sun et al., 2024).
2.6 Advanced Deep Learning Models and Hybrid Approaches
Beyond conventional CNNs, recent studies have explored hybrid and advanced architectures to enhance disease detection performance. Attention mechanisms, transformers, and ensemble models have been introduced to capture global and local features more effectively (Zhang et al., 2024).
Sharma et al. (2025) proposed an ensemble model combining ResNet50 and MobileNetV2, achieving over 99% accuracy in tomato disease classification. Vision Transformer (ViT)-based models have also shown promising results by capturing long-range dependencies in leaf images (Shehu et al., 2025).
Hybrid approaches that combine deep learning with traditional machine learning classifiers have further improved robustness and interpretability (Khan et al., 2024). These models often integrate handcrafted features with deep features to enhance classification performance.
2.7 Datasets Used in Tomato Disease Detection
Publicly available datasets such as PlantVillage have been extensively used for training and evaluating tomato disease detection models. The PlantVillage dataset contains labeled images of healthy and diseased tomato leaves under controlled conditions (Hughes & Salathé, 2015).
Although PlantVillage has facilitated rapid research progress, several authors have noted its limitations in representing real-world farming environments (Casanova et al., 2023). Consequently, recent studies have incorporated field-acquired images to improve model generalization.

2.8 Performance Evaluation Metrics
Model performance in tomato disease detection studies is commonly evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrices. Accuracy is the most widely reported metric; however, precision and recall provide deeper insights into model reliability, particularly in imbalanced datasets (Sokolova & Lapalme, 2009).
Most recent studies report classification accuracies above 90%, with some achieving over 99% under controlled conditions (Islam et al., 2024). However, performance often decreases when models are tested on real-world field data.
2.9 Limitations of Existing Studies
Despite significant advancements, existing tomato disease detection systems face several challenges. Many models are trained on laboratory-controlled datasets, limiting their robustness in real-world environments. Variations in lighting, background clutter, occlusion, and disease severity remain major issues (Casanova et al., 2023).
Additionally, most studies focus solely on leaf images and do not consider other plant parts or environmental factors such as soil conditions and weather, which influence disease development (Jabbar et al., 2025).
2.10 Research Gap
From the reviewed literature, it is evident that while deep learning models have achieved high accuracy in tomato disease detection, there is a need for systems that are robust, efficient, and suitable for real-world deployment. Many existing models are computationally intensive and lack adaptability to field conditions.
This study seeks to address these gaps by developing an optimized deep learning-based tomato disease detection model that balances accuracy and computational efficiency while leveraging image preprocessing and transfer learning techniques.

METHODOLOGY

This chapter describes the research methodology adopted for the development of an intelligent system for detecting diseases in tomato plants using image processing and deep learning techniques. It explains the overall research design, data collection methods, system architecture, model development process, training and evaluation procedures, and tools used in the study. The methodology is designed to ensure that the proposed system is accurate, efficient, and suitable for practical application in real-world agricultural environments.
3.2 Research Design
The research adopts an experimental and developmental research design. The experimental approach is used to train and evaluate a deep learning model on tomato4 leaf images, while the developmental approach focuses on designing and implementing an automated tomato disease detection system. This design is appropriate because the study involves building, testing, and validating a computational model rather than conducting surveys or interviews. The research workflow includes data acquisition, image preprocessing, model training, performance evaluation, and result analysis.
3.3 Dataset Description and Data Collection
The dataset used in this study consists of digital images of tomato leaves, including both healthy and diseased samples. The images represent common tomato diseases such as early blight, late blight, septoria leaf spot, bacterial spot, and leaf mold. The dataset may be obtained from publicly available agricultural image repositories such as 
the PlantVillage dataset, as well as additional images collected from online agricultural resources.
Each image in the dataset is labeled according to its disease class to enable supervised learning. The dataset is divided into three subsets: training, validation, and testing datasets. The training dataset is used to train the deep learning model, the validation dataset is used to fine-tune model parameters and prevent over fitting, and the testing dataset is used to evaluate the final performance of the model.

3.5 Image Preprocessing
Image preprocessing is a critical step aimed at improving image quality and ensuring consistency across the dataset. The preprocessing techniques applied in this study include image resizing, normalization, noise reduction, and data augmentation.
Image resizing is performed to ensure that all images have uniform dimensions compatible with the input requirements of the deep learning model. Normalization is applied to scale pixel values to a standard range, which helps improve model convergence during training. Noise reduction techniques are used to minimize irrelevant visual artifacts that may interfere with feature extraction.
Data augmentation techniques such as rotation, flipping, zooming, and brightness adjustment are applied to increase dataset diversity and reduce overfitting. These techniques enable the model to learn invariant features and generalize better to unseen data.

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is employed as the core classification model due to its effectiveness in image recognition tasks. The CNN architecture consists of multiple convolutional layers, pooling layers, and fully connected layers.
The convolutional layers are responsible for extracting low-level and high-level features such as edges, textures, and disease patterns from tomato leaf images. Pooling layers reduce spatial dimensions and computational complexity while preserving important features. Fully connected layers perform the final classification based on extracted features.
To improve performance and reduce training time, transfer learning may be applied using a pre-trained model such as VGG16, ResNet50, or MobileNet. The final layers of the pre-trained model are fine-tuned using the tomato leaf dataset to adapt the model to the specific task of tomato disease detection.

The dataset is split into training and validation sets, and the model is trained over multiple epochs. The performance of the trained model is evaluated using standard classification metrics. These include accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.
Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified images. Confusion matrices are also used to visualize model performance across different disease classes.
3.10 Model Architecture
The proposed tomato disease detection system is based on a deep learning architecture utilizing a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The CNN model is designed to automatically learn and extract discriminative features from tomato leaf images and classify them into healthy or diseased categories. The architecture is structured to achieve high classification accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency for potential real-world deployment.
The model architecture consists of an input layer, multiple convolutional and pooling layers for feature extraction, followed by fully connected layers for classification. Each component of the architecture contributes to the transformation of raw image data into meaningful disease predictions.

Fig 3.2 Model Architecture for Plant Disease Detection Using Image Preprocessing and Deep Learning

Input Layer
The input layer receives preprocessed tomato leaf images. All images are resized to a fixed dimension to ensure uniformity across the dataset and normalized to standardize pixel intensity values. This preprocessing step helps stabilize training, accelerates convergence, and ensures that the model processes all images consistently.
Convolutional Layers
The convolutional layers are responsible for automatic feature extraction. These layers apply multiple learnable filters to the input images to detect important visual patterns such as edges, color variations, texture changes, and lesion formations associated with tomato diseases.
As the depth of the network increases, the convolutional layers learn higher-level features that represent complex disease characteristics, including spots, blights, and discoloration patterns. Each convolutional layer is followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, which introduces non-linearity into the network and improves learning efficiency by mitigating the vanishing gradient problem.
Pooling Layers
Pooling layers are incorporated after selected convolutional layers to reduce the spatial dimensions of the extracted feature maps. Max pooling is employed to retain the most significant features while discarding redundant information. This process reduces computational complexity, enhances translation invariance, and helps prevent overfitting.
Dropout Layers
To further address overfitting, dropout layers are introduced within the network. During training, dropout randomly deactivates a fraction of neurons, forcing the model to learn more robust and generalized feature representations. This improves the model's ability to perform well on unseen tomato leaf images.
Fully Connected Layers
The fully connected layers receive the flattened output from the final convolutional layer. These layers perform high-level reasoning by combining the extracted features and mapping them to the disease classification categories. ReLU activation functions are used in the hidden fully connected layers to maintain learning capacity and improve convergence.
Output Layer
The output layer consists of neurons corresponding to the number of tomato disease classes, including healthy leaves. A Softmax activation function is applied to generate probability scores for each class. The class with the highest probability is selected as the final prediction of the system.
Model Optimization
The CNN model is trained using an optimization algorithm such as the Adam optimizer, which adaptively adjusts learning rates to improve convergence speed and stability. The categorical cross-entropy loss function is used to measure the discrepancy between predicted and actual class labels. Model performance is evaluated using validation data to ensure proper generalization and to prevent overfitting.
Justification of the Architecture
This architecture is chosen due to its ability to automatically learn relevant disease features from images without manual feature engineering. The combination of convolutional layers, pooling operations, dropout regularization, and fully connected layers provides a robust framework for accurate and efficient tomato disease detection.
